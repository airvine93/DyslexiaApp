# 100 video ideas

Made by `node render.mjs manifest` from `src/ideas.js` and `src/design.js`. Edit those, not this file.

Every video has the same five acts: a hook, a short lesson, the lesson turning hard to read with a question, the change into a reading font (plus any extra features), and a closing line.

## What changes between videos

| Setting | What it tests | Levels |
|---|---|---|
| type | What is being taught | word, origin, quote, fact, phrase |
| hook | Opening line: a label or a question | label, question |
| scene | Three.js background | orbs, dust, letters, ribbons |
| theme | Background and text colours | navy, ink, dusk, cream |
| accent | Pastel highlight colour | rose, peach, butter, mint, sky, periwinkle, orchid, apricot |
| hard | How the text is made hard to read | cramped, crowd, faint, glare, swim |
| ask | Wording of the question | a1, a2, a3, a4 |
| reveal | How the letters change into the reading font | morph, scatter, flip, sweep, wave |
| font | Reading font shown | opendyslexic, lexend, atkinson, andika, comicneue, readex |
| feat | Features shown after the font | font, bionic, tint, ruler, all |
| cta | Closing line | c1, c2, c3, c4 |
| pace | Speed | fast, medium, slow |
| cam | Camera movement | still, drift, push |

The type follows the idea. Every other setting is dealt out evenly and shuffled, so each level is used about the same number of times and no two settings always travel together.

## Lessons

The paragraph each video teaches, which is also the text that turns hard to read and is then restyled.

1. **petrichor** The pleasant, earthy scent that rises when rain lands on ground that has been dry for a while. Plant oils and soil bacteria make it, and falling raindrops lift it into the air.
2. **serendipity** Finding something good that you were not looking for. It is more than luck: it takes the wit to notice the lucky thing and see its worth. Penicillin was found this way.
3. **mellifluous** Describes a sound that is sweet, smooth and pleasant to hear, flowing without a rough edge. It is used mostly for voices, music and speech, and often as praise for a singer.
4. **ephemeral** Lasting for a very short time, and often more precious because of it. A mayfly, a rainbow, cherry blossom and a message that vanishes after reading are all ephemeral. The opposite is permanent.
5. **defenestrate** To throw someone or something out of a window. It is mostly said with a wink now, and also means removing someone from power suddenly, as when a party defenestrates its leader.
6. **apricity** The warmth of the sun on a cold winter day, felt on your skin while the air stays sharp. It names a small pleasure, which is why people who learn it keep it.
7. **susurrus** A soft, continuous whispering, murmuring or rustling sound. Think of wind moving through dry leaves, a quiet tide on sand, or low voices in the next room. The word itself sounds like what it describes.
8. **halcyon** Calm, happy and untroubled, always said of a time in the past. Halcyon days are a golden stretch you remember fondly, such as a long childhood summer, often with a sense that it cannot return.
9. **ineffable** Too great, too intense or too sacred to be put into words. People reach for it when describing awe, grief, love or beauty, at the exact moment when ordinary language stops being enough.
10. **overmorrow** The day after tomorrow, in one word. If today is Monday, overmorrow is Wednesday. It is old and rare now, but it does a job that modern English needs four words for.
11. **tsundoku** The habit of buying books and letting them pile up unread. It is Japanese, and it carries no shame: the pile is a promise to your future self, not a failure of your present one.
12. **komorebi** Sunlight filtering through the leaves of trees, and the shifting pattern of light and shadow it makes on the ground. It is a Japanese word for a sight English needs a whole sentence to describe.
13. **saudade** A deep, bittersweet longing for someone or something that is gone and may never come back. It is sadness and gratitude at once: missing a person, a place or a time because it mattered.
14. **hygge** A feeling of cosy contentment and ease, usually shared. Candles, warm socks, a slow meal with friends and no reason to hurry all count. It is Danish, and it is said roughly as hoo-gah.
15. **sonorous** Having a deep, full and resonant sound that seems to fill the space around it. A church bell, a cello and a rich speaking voice are sonorous. It can also describe grand, impressive language.
16. **lethologica** The temporary inability to remember a word that you know perfectly well. You can often recall its first letter, its length or its rhythm, but the word itself stays on the tip of your tongue.
17. **quixotic** Pursuing lofty, romantic ideals with no regard for how practical they are. A quixotic plan is noble and doomed at once. It can be an insult or a compliment, depending on who is speaking.
18. **sesquipedalian** Describes a very long word, or a person who is fond of using them. It often carries a gentle tease, aimed at writing that picks the grand word where a plain one would do.
19. **eloquent** Able to express ideas clearly, fluently and persuasively. It is less about long words than about the right ones. A look, a silence or a gesture can be eloquent too, when it says enough.
20. **luminous** Giving off light, or seeming to glow from within. A watch dial and a jellyfish are luminous. So is clear, radiant writing, a painting full of light, or a face lit up by good news.
21. **muscle** The Romans thought a flexing muscle looked like a small mouse moving under the skin. The Latin word musculus means exactly that.
22. **disaster** From the Italian disastro: dis, bad, and astro, star. People once blamed calamities on the unlucky position of a planet or a star.
23. **goodbye** Over the 1500s the phrase was squeezed into godbwye. The first part then shifted to good, copying good day and good night.
24. **clue** A clew was a ball of yarn. In Greek myth Theseus used one to find his way out of the Labyrinth, so a clew came to mean anything that guides you to an answer.
25. **window** From the Old Norse vindauga: vindr, wind, and auga, eye. Early windows were plain openings in a wall or roof that let smoke out and wind in.
26. **daisy** The Old English name was the day’s eye, because the flower opens its petals at dawn and closes them again at dusk.
27. **companion** From the Latin com, with, and panis, bread. A companion was the person you broke bread with. Company comes from the same root.
28. **robot** It first appeared in a Czech play of 1920 about factory-made workers. The writer’s brother suggested the word, from robota.
29. **quarantine** From the Venetian quaranta giorni. During the plague, ships arriving in Venice had to wait at anchor for forty days before anyone could come ashore.
30. **nice** From the Latin nescius, not knowing. Over seven centuries the word drifted from silly to fussy to precise, and only then to pleasant.
31. **silly** In Old English it meant happy or blessed. It slid to innocent, then to pitiable, then to weak, and at last to foolish.
32. **nightmare** A mare was an evil spirit in old folklore, said to sit on the chests of sleepers and bring bad dreams.
33. **whisky** From the Gaelic uisge beatha. English speakers wore the phrase down over time until only whisky was left.
34. **checkmate** Shah mat travelled with the game of chess through Arabic and French into English. Shah, king, also gives us the word check.
35. **algebra** Al-jabr comes from the title of a book written in Baghdad around the year 820 by al-Khwarizmi, whose own name gave us the word algorithm.
36. **sarcasm** The Greek sarkazein meant to strip off flesh like a dog, then to bite the lip in rage, then to sneer. Sharp words have always had teeth.
37. **tragedy** From the Greek tragos, goat, and oide, song. Nobody is sure why. A goat may have been the prize in early Athenian drama contests.
38. **candidate** In ancient Rome, men standing for office wore a toga rubbed bright white with chalk. The Latin candidus, shining white, also gives us candid.
39. **shampoo** Champo first meant a massage. The word reached Britain in the 1700s and only came to mean washing hair about a century later.
40. **emoji** It is Japanese: e, picture, and moji, character. The likeness to the English word emoticon is a happy accident.
41. **Brevity is the soul of wit.** The joke is on the speaker. Polonius says this in the middle of a long, rambling speech, and Shakespeare lets the audience notice.
42. **Words, words, words.** Polonius asks Hamlet what he is reading, and this is the whole reply. Three words that say he is tired of being watched and tired of empty talk.
43. **The pen is mightier than the sword.** The idea is far older, but this is the wording that stuck. Almost everybody knows the line and almost nobody remembers who wrote it.
44. **Language is the dress of thought.** Johnson wrote one of the first great English dictionaries, published in 1755. He believed that how you say a thing shapes how it is heard.
45. **The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.** A philosopher’s way of saying that every new word you learn makes your world a little bigger.
46. **Curiouser and curiouser!** Alice is so surprised that, as the book puts it, she quite forgot how to speak good English. Carroll loved bending words until they squeaked.
47. **A word is dead when it is said, some say. I say it just begins to live that day.** Dickinson wrote nearly 1,800 poems and published only a handful in her lifetime. Most were found in her room after she died.
48. **There is no frigate like a book to take us lands away.** A frigate is a fast sailing ship. Dickinson rarely left her home town, and travelled a long way by reading.
49. **Hope is the thing with feathers that perches in the soul.** She turns a feeling into a small bird that keeps singing through the storm and never asks for a crumb in return.
50. **Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested.** His advice still holds. You do not owe every book the same attention. Skim some, read some, and live with a few.
51. **Reading maketh a full man, conference a ready man, and writing an exact man.** Conference here means conversation. Read to fill your mind, talk to sharpen it, and write to find out what you really think.
52. **A little learning is a dangerous thing.** Often misquoted as a little knowledge. His point was that a small taste makes us overconfident, and deeper study makes us humble again.
53. **To err is human; to forgive, divine.** Pope published this at twenty-three, in a poem about how to judge other people’s writing fairly and kindly.
54. **It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.** This is only the start of the opening sentence, which runs on for well over a hundred words before it reaches a full stop.
55. **Call me Ishmael.** Three words, and one of the most famous openings in English. Notice he does not say it is his name, only what to call him.
56. **All the world’s a stage, and all the men and women merely players.** The speech goes on to split a life into seven ages, from the infant to the very old. Player was the word for an actor.
57. **We are such stuff as dreams are made on.** Often misquoted as made of. Prospero says it as his magic show melts away, in one of the last plays Shakespeare wrote.
58. **The best words in the best order.** His full definition was a pair. Prose is words in their best order. Poetry is the best words in the best order.
59. **Words are, of course, the most powerful drug used by mankind.** Kipling said this in a speech to surgeons in London. He told them that words do not only carry meaning, they change how people feel.
60. **Tell all the truth but tell it slant.** Her advice is to come at hard truths from the side, gently. The truth, she writes, must dazzle gradually.
61. **typewriter** On a standard QWERTY keyboard all ten letters sit on the top row. So do the letters of proprietor, perpetuity and repertoire.
62. **rhythm** The letter y does the work of a vowel here. Other words that manage without the usual five include myth, lynx, crypt and gym.
63. **uncopyrightable** A word with no repeated letters is called an isogram. Dermatoglyphics, the study of fingerprints, is another with fifteen.
64. **almost** So do begin, chimp, ghost and biopsy. Words like this are rare, and the longer the word, the rarer it gets.
65. **bookkeeper** Double o, double k, double e. Bookkeeper and bookkeeping are the only common English words with three pairs of double letters back to back.
66. **queue** Take away the last four letters and you still say it the same way. The word is French for tail, which is what a line of people looks like.
67. **the tittle** It comes from the Latin for a small mark. Some people think the phrase to a T began as to a tittle, meaning down to the smallest detail.
68. **&** Children ended the alphabet with: and, per se, and. Said quickly, that slurred into the word ampersand.
69. **E** It turns up in roughly one of every eight letters you read. In 1939 Ernest Vincent Wright published Gadsby, a 50,000 word novel without a single e.
70. **the** It makes up roughly one word in every twenty on a typical page. The top ten also includes be, to, of, and, a and in.
71. **run** Editors of the Oxford English Dictionary counted 645 senses for the verb run, taking the record from set.
72. **ye olde** The y stood in for an old letter called thorn, which made the th sound. Printers without a thorn used y, and readers later forgot.
73. **OK** In 1839 a Boston newspaper used it as a jokey short form of oll korrect, a deliberate misspelling of all correct. It is now understood almost everywhere.
74. **dreamt** It is the only common English word that does, along with its relatives undreamt and daydreamt.
75. **strengths** It is among the longest English words with a single vowel, and one of the longest you can say in a single syllable.
76. **pangram** The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog is the famous one. Sphinx of black quartz, judge my vow does it in twenty-nine letters.
77. **palindrome** Level, kayak and racecar all work. So does a whole sentence: A man, a plan, a canal, Panama.
78. **your eyes** They jump. Each hop is called a saccade, and you only take in words during the short pauses in between, a few letters at a time.
79. **one in ten** Dyslexia mainly affects reading and spelling. It has nothing to do with intelligence, and many dyslexic people are strong visual and creative thinkers.
80. **octothorpe** Engineers at Bell Labs are said to have coined it in the 1960s for the telephone keypad. Octo is for its eight points.
81. **a murmuration** Thousands of starlings turn together in the evening sky like one shape. The name copies the murmur of all those wings.
82. **an exaltation** It comes from a hunting book printed in 1486, the Book of Saint Albans, which also lists a murder of crows and a pride of lions.
83. **a flamboyance** Flamingo and flamboyant both go back to words for flame. The birds get their pink from the shrimp and algae they eat.
84. **break the ice** Small boats broke river ice so that trade could start again. By the 1600s it also meant easing the first awkward moments between strangers.
85. **once in a blue moon** Today a blue moon usually means a second full moon in one calendar month. That happens about once every two or three years.
86. **spill the beans** It appeared in American slang in the early 1900s. The story about ancient Greeks voting with beans is a nice one, but there is no proof.
87. **under the weather** It is probably a sailor’s phrase. A seasick sailor was sent below deck, out of the weather, to recover.
88. **bite the bullet** The story goes that soldiers bit on a bullet during surgery without anaesthetic. Kipling used the phrase in a novel in 1891.
89. **affect or effect?** Rain affects your mood. Rain has an effect on your mood. If you can put the or an in front of it, you want effect.
90. **stationary or stationery?** Stationery with an e is paper, pens and envelopes. Stationary with an a means standing still, like a parked car.
91. **their, there, they’re** Their shows belonging. There points to a place. They’re is short for they are. Say they are out loud, and if it fits, use the apostrophe.
92. **lose or loose?** You lose your keys. A loose tooth wobbles. Lose rhymes with choose, and loose rhymes with goose.
93. **desert or dessert?** A desert is dry sand. Dessert is pudding. To desert someone, with the stress on the end, is to abandon them.
94. **complement or compliment?** Complement shares its first six letters with complete. A compliment with an i is the kind thing that I say to you.
95. **she sells seashells** Tongue twisters work by packing nearly identical sounds together, here s and sh, so your tongue has to keep switching between them.
96. **red lorry, yellow lorry** The sounds r and l are made with the tongue in almost the same place, so swapping quickly between them trips nearly everybody up.
97. **Peter Piper** The rhyme was printed in London in 1813 in a book of tongue twisters, one for each letter of the alphabet. A peck is about nine litres.
98. **the whole nine yards** Nobody knows where it comes from. Guesses include cloth, concrete and machine-gun belts, and none of them has been proved.
99. **principal or principle?** A principal is the head of a school, or the main thing. A principle is a rule or a belief that you stick to.
100. **onomatopoeia** Buzz, hiss, splash, cuckoo, murmur. The name is Greek for making a name, and it is much harder to spell than any of its examples.
